I won't deny it's cool that the internet can now help people like me code. (Me, a person who cried thru every TA office hour of my intro to python class! I made this site with (a lot of) help from my girlfriend and a i). Ultimately I will use the tools I critique and I guess we are all doing that all the time. The problem are the wealthy and their companies that wield these tools for capitalist evils while ignoring the impacts on regular people and the environment. The technology itself is often rather mesmerizing.

___

My sister’s instagram account disappeared. I don’t know why. I went through the process of “memorializing” it after her death, which included sending in a death certificate to Meta to prove she was dead. With sufficient proof, they added a tag underneath her profile name and photo that read, “Remembering”. According to Meta policy, that shouldn’t have changed without another family member deleting the account. I suppose that’s possible. I didn’t do it and no one asked me if that should happen (I am arguably the only person in my boomer-family with generationally-induced emotional ties to instagram, after all). Maybe it was a glitch. Maybe there’s another account setting I just don’t know about. Maybe they changed their policy. But the one record of herself, the photos from her life which she consented to displaying to the world, are totally gone.

When my sister died, I carefully sifted through thousands of photos I had saved to my phone and digitally deposited each one that featured her into a shared iCloud folder called "Lara" that I shared only with myself. As time went by, I started making more albums.

Adding the photos to the cloud, deleting them from my phone. Something about organizing my life and hiding all the evidence without destroying all the proof was soothing. Confronting all of these photos made me question if I indeed wanted all of them, and how I even had a hoard in the first place.

My grandma used to up her physical photo albums and flip through the pages. There were maybe 2-3 photos from entire decades. One photo of herself when she was 27. There is something so freeing about the sparsity. To be pleasantly surprised and reminded. Instead, if I am lucky enough to have a death bed to lie in, I fear I will be sifting through pimply selfies from a time in my life when I had a busted haircut and an even more busted camera and screenshots of Mamdanistan memes to find the one photo of a farm at sunset taken in 2023.

It would be nice to have to work a little harder to see what I chose to capture. To have the luxury and space to decide what we take photos of and furthermore, what I want to take with me. Instead, Apple, Meta, etc. are profiting from a manufactured sense of desperation caused by a narcissistic obsession with our own lives, making us believe we must constantly save, and subsequently document and share our own lives for them to be worth living.

I got a phone that has a camera and I put that phone in my pocket and now I habitually take that phone out to try to capture and prove that I am alive. I have filled my phone with beauty and slop and relationships and I see it all played back to me whenever I choose. And maybe periodically I go through and see, is there anything exciting here? Is there anything that proves my life is interesting, fun, full of love, joyous, compelling, real? I pick a mix of beauty and slop and call it a June dump for all my instagram followers and feel disappointed when people don’t engage with it. I start to believe it is a reflection on the quality of my life and not simply because I started with roses instead of my face.

I am wary of the dump. I am wary of this handshake deal between Apple and Meta that has convinced us we should capture everything now to be shared later. We curate and present our lives for consumption online, tricked into believing we have ownership, privacy, and community. I have caught myself at a Chappell Roan concert and had identifiable stress about not having enough photos or the right photos to prove to people how much fun I had. In my anxiety to be interesting I am most certainly a fine example of exactly who these billionaires rely on. I am what's needed for them to execute their business-model that is actively destroying the planet and its people.

I spent the past several months going through every photo on my phone from my 26th birthday - my 29th. I organized these photos into random categories that do not follow any particular pattern. Some are overarching for three years (i.e., "dogs"), some are snapshots of moments in time ("Cam and Adele in NYC 2025"). There is inconsistent overlap - some are in multiple folders, some should be, but only appear once. It is messy, incoherent, and in this exact form, not at all replicable. After filing or deleting every photo, I made a website that shows the albums. The photos themselves are saved to a flash drive I now keep in my underwear drawer. If you're reading this, the photos themselves have been deleted from the cloud.

Why? I think I wanted to experiment with sharing a bit about myself in a way that doesn’t necessarily make sense, a way that is the opposite of optimized for social media, a way that is entirely purposeless and not in line with an app’s goals or business plan. I like knowing that the way I organized my photos wouldn't make sense to any learn-ed machine, it only makes sense to my brain, following a sophologic that's just for me. I am a product of my generation and the companies that shaped us, but I would like to exercise a bit of agency here using a tool that is now constantly deployed to control, endeavor to create something not optimized for mass consumption.

I won’t act as if I will magically start engaging with instagram (or google, Meta, etc.) in an entirely different way. But I’m trying ever so slightly to ask myself whether I am acting in my interest or for that of a corporation. And I will try to make things for the thrill of it, reminding myself that posting doesn’t mean I’m happy and likewise, can’t make me so.

It feels sill to be sad my sister's instagram account is gone. Ultimately, it is a reminder that we have no control over how we live on - digitally or otherwise. To be in the moment is to be alive.
